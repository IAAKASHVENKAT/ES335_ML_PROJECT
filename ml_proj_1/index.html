1. The question

Can a store manager forecast next week's sales using only what is known at the start of that week, and does a machine-learning model do this better than a simple rule?

  • One row = one store in one week.
  • Target = Weekly_Sales (dollars).
  • Prediction time = the beginning of the week. We know the store, the calendar week, whether it is a holiday week, the previous week's temperature, fuel price, CPI and unemployment, and last week's sales for the same store.
  • Claim limit: we predict sales, we do not explain what causes them. Temperature or CPI being useful for prediction does not mean they drive sales.

Walmart weekly sales dataset (Kaggle). The file is included in this repository as Walmart.csv (it is also on Kaggle, which needs a free login).

3. Preprocessing

  • Lag feature: last week's sales for the same store (lag1). The first week of each store has no lag and is dropped.
  • Lagged context: temperature, fuel price, CPI and unemployment from the previous week are used instead of values from the week being forecast. This avoids relying on information that may not yet be available at the beginning of the forecast week.
  • Week of year from the date, to catch seasonality.
  • Scaling of numeric columns and one-hot encoding of Store. Both are inside a scikit-learn Pipeline, so they are fit on training rows only and cannot leak information from validation or test.

Statistical reasoning behind the choices

    4. Baselines and methods

    MethodIdea and why it is here
    Naive (last week)Predict this week = last week. Sales are strongly persistent, so this is a hard baseline to beat.
    Store averagePredict each store's mean training sales. Ignores time completely.
    Ridge regressionLinear model with L2 penalty. Assumes roughly linear effects, simple and stable.
    Random forestAverage of many trees. Can capture non-linear effects and interactions, makes no scaling assumptions.
    Gradient boostingTrees built one after another to fix earlier errors. Often strongest on tabular data.

    More complex is not assumed to be better. The naive rule gets the same chance to win.

    5. Evaluation design

    Random shuffling would let the model train on weeks after the ones it is tested on, which is not what happens in real forecasting. So the split is by time and was fixed before comparing models:

    • Hyperparameters are tuned only on the validation block.
    • The model type is also chosen on validation.
    • The final models are refit on train + validation and scored on the test block once.

    Metrics: MAE (average dollar error, easy to read), RMSE (punishes big misses more), WMAPE (total absolute error divided by total sales, a percentage that stays defined and comparable across large and small stores). We avoid plain MAPE and R² as headline numbers.

    8. Where the model fails

    In the test block the model beats the naive rule overall, but in the 45 holiday-week rows it is no better than the naive rule. Only 450 of the 6,435 rows are holiday weeks, so the model has little to learn the holiday jump from.

    Four of the five largest errors are Store 14 in ordinary (non-holiday) weeks between June and September 2012, and Store 14 has the largest average test error of all stores. The model over-predicts three of those weeks and under-predicts one, so this is not a simple bias. We have not found the cause; the weekly swings at that store are larger than last week's sales and the other features can explain. This is a limit of the model, not a finding about Store 14.

    9. Limitations

    • Only about three years of weekly data, so each calendar week is seen at most twice in training. Seasonal patterns are learned from very few examples.
    • The test block is short (about 22 weeks) and covers one stretch of time, so the scores are one estimate with real uncertainty. We bootstrapped over weeks for the gap to the naive rule, but not for every number in the tables, and a single split is not cross-validation.
    • Using last week's sales assumes we have it. This is fine for next-week forecasting but not for forecasting several weeks ahead.
    • Macro columns (CPI, unemployment) are regional and slow-moving. They are not a causal explanation of sales.
    • Holiday weeks are few, so errors on them are noisy.

    Key choices and why

    • Time-based split, not random. A random split would train on weeks after the ones it is tested on, which cannot happen in real forecasting and would make scores look better than they are.
    • Last week's sales as a feature. It is known when next week's forecast is made, so it is allowed. It is also the strongest signal, so we compare against a baseline that uses only it.
    • Preprocessing inside a Pipeline. Scaling and encoding are fit on training rows only, so validation and test data cannot leak into them.
    • Validation for choices, held-out test for evaluation. Model settings and the winning model were chosen using validation data. We then evaluated on the later test block. The separate ablation experiment is reported as a post-hoc diagnostic, not as a basis for choosing the model.
    • WMAPE next to MAE and RMSE. It gives an error as a percentage of total sales and stays meaningful across large and small stores.

    Reasoning about small changes

    We refit the chosen model with one group of features removed and scored it on the test block. This is a check for understanding only: nothing was chosen using it.

      If we changed the split to 60/20/20 (edit the two cutoffs 0.70 and 0.85 in train.py): we would expect a longer validation block to make tuning more reliable and a longer test block to narrow the bootstrap interval, at the cost of less training data and fewer seasonal examples per week of the year. We have not run this, so it is an expectation, not a result.
